Dead networks aren't poor. They're demoralized.

I built an agent-based model to test a hypothesis: communication networks — the kind Conway's Law describes — need active boundary conditions to stay alive. Not rules that prevent transactions. Boundaries that encourage them.

Setup: 100 agents, 300 rounds of bilateral transactions. Willingness to transact is driven by morale — a function of relative wealth position. Wealthier agents carry a structural edge (information, leverage, network position). Standard dynamics.

Three scenarios. Unambiguous results.

---

NO BOUNDARIES
Gini: 0.88 | Morale: 0.52 | Transactions: 21,908

Wealth concentrates. The majority loses morale. They stop transacting. The network goes silent — not because agents are forbidden from participating, but because they've lost the will to.

---

SOFT BOUNDARIES (Redistribution)
Gini: 0.40 | Morale: 0.75 | Transactions: 26,562

Progressive tax on extreme wealth + a floor for the bottom. Keeps the game playable. But reactive — fixes damage after it happens.

---

ACTIVE BOUNDARIES (Transaction Incentives)
Gini: 0.38 | Morale: 0.92 | Transactions: 33,946

Three specific mechanisms:

1. Participation Morale Floor — agents who transacted last round can't have morale drop below a threshold. Showing up is itself rewarded.

2. Structural Advantage Dampening — the wealthy still win more often, but their edge compounds at 1/3 the rate. Competition stays, runaway dominance doesn't.

3. Network Vitality Feedback — when transaction volume is high, everyone gets a morale boost. The system rewards its own health.

Plus a cross-wealth diversity dividend: transacting across large wealth gaps earns both parties a bonus, weighted toward the poorer party.

---

Result: 62% more transactions than the free market. Highest morale. Lowest inequality. Not through restriction — through incentive design.

The Conway's Law insight: your network's output mirrors its communication structure. But communication requires willingness. Willingness requires morale. Morale requires believing participation is worth it.

Three principles:

→ Reward participation itself, not just outcomes
→ Dampen structural advantage compounding — don't eliminate competition, prevent runaway dominance
→ Build feedback loops between network health and individual benefit

Boundary conditions aren't constraints on freedom. They're the infrastructure that keeps freedom meaningful.
