In 2017, I ran a game theory simulation on Venezuela's refugee-as-leverage strategy, and the equilibrium it converges to is worth paying attention to.

The scenario: Venezuela directs refugee flows specifically toward nations holding lines of credit in its oil sector. The implicit pressure — accept these people, or face mounting diplomatic and reputational costs while holding a depreciating asset.

I modeled this as a 4-player repeated game (500 Monte Carlo simulations, 50 rounds each) with reinforcement learning. Players: Venezuela, Creditor Countries, International Organizations, and NGOs.

The result: Cooperative Equilibrium — the leverage converts.

Venezuela converges to "Flood Creditor Nations" (74%) — directing mass refugee flows toward credit-holders.

Creditor Countries converge to "Accept & Yield Credit" (55%) — surrendering credit positions rather than absorbing compounding reputational damage.

International Orgs converge to "No Intervention" (62%) — staying out once the bilateral pressure resolves itself.

NGOs converge to "Silent" (55%) — disengaging when creditors already capitulate without needing external pressure.

What this means:

1. Refugee flows are functioning as geopolitical leverage. When the cost of holding a position (reputation erosion + diplomatic isolation) exceeds the credit line's value over time, rational actors yield. This isn't humanitarian — it's economic pressure wearing a humanitarian mask.

2. The leverage works precisely because it compounds. Each round of rejection increases reputational damage, making the next round's calculus worse for creditors. It's a ratchet, not a one-shot game.

3. International institutions become irrelevant when bilateral pressure is sufficient. The simulation shows orgs and NGOs rationally disengage — their intervention adds cost without changing outcomes that are already converging.

4. The 27% who renegotiate point to a middle path. Not all creditors fully yield — some find structured deals that preserve partial credit value. This suggests policy space exists for managed transitions rather than total capitulation.

5. Venezuela pays almost nothing to maintain this strategy. Directing refugee flows costs less than the credit positions it extracts. Asymmetric leverage at its most efficient.

The uncomfortable implication: the equilibrium is stable because it's rational for everyone. Creditors aren't being irrational by yielding — they're correctly computing that holding becomes more expensive than releasing. Game theory doesn't tell us what's right. It tells us what converges.
